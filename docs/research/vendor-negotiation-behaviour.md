# How real vendors behave when a buyer negotiates price and terms

Research input for the rule-based vendor simulator. Compiled 2026-10-01 from web sources. Every bullet ends with its source. Items I could not verify are flagged in **[UNVERIFIED]** or in the "Gaps" list at the end. No quote is invented; quotes are kept under 15 words and are taken from pages I opened. Where I could only read a search-result summary, I say so.

## Summary

- Suppliers rarely open at their real limit. They anchor high, justify with a cost story, then move in small, shrinking steps. The final price tends to land near the midpoint of the last two offers.
- Resistance is usually a mix of real constraints and tactics: "that is my final offer", "I need to check with my manager", a deadline, or "there is nothing more I can do". Research suggests many ultimatums are not final; repeated ultimatums usually are.
- Under margin pressure, suppliers trade rather than just cut: scope, quality, service, volume commitments, payment terms, contract length, delivery. Research on automotive suppliers shows they also quietly cut quality and service after big price cuts.
- Stingy concessions (high demands, small moves) raise the chance of no deal, mostly through anger and the reduced value conceded, not through broken fairness norms.
- In large field data (eBay Best Offer), only about one third of bargaining ends in immediate agreement, bargaining is short (max 3 offers each, 48-hour expiry), and split-the-difference is the dominant norm.
- No reliable open source gives hours/days timelines for B2B supplier negotiation, or B2B no-deal rates. Treat any such numbers in the simulator as tunable design parameters, not facts.
- India: the strongest hard facts are legal (MSMED Act s.15/16, Income-tax s.43B(h)): 45-day payment cap for micro and small suppliers, and compound interest on default. Cultural claims (relationship, hierarchy, indirectness) come from thinner academic sources.

## 1. How suppliers show disapproval or resistance

- **Take It Or Leave It**: one party "will state that the offer is final and there is no movement"; CIPS says it is a harsh ploy "often used when relationships are not strategic". [CIPS Negotiation Tools Guidance Notes](https://udntho.files.cmp.optimizely.com/download/assets/CIPS+Negotiation+Tools+Guidance+notes.pdf/490ffa3ebafd11f099f70e9f7034f9df)
- **Mother Hubbard**: the party says there is nothing left they can do or offer, "that may or may not be the case". [CIPS Guidance Notes](https://udntho.files.cmp.optimizely.com/download/assets/CIPS+Negotiation+Tools+Guidance+notes.pdf/490ffa3ebafd11f099f70e9f7034f9df)
- **Lack of authority**: the party says they cannot approve a deal over a certain value or agree to sell that low; the intent is that the other side backs down instead of waiting for approval. [CIPS Guidance Notes](https://udntho.files.cmp.optimizely.com/download/assets/CIPS+Negotiation+Tools+Guidance+notes.pdf/490ffa3ebafd11f099f70e9f7034f9df)
- **"Let me talk to my manager"** is the classic seller phrasing for limited authority; it delays, adds uncertainty about approval time, and can signal that no more concessions are possible. Karrass notes it can be a real organisational constraint or a manoeuvre. [Karrass, understanding authority](https://www.karrass.com/blog/negotiation-tactic-understanding-authority)
- **Threat types** named by Harvard PON include ultimatums ("That's our final offer. Take it or leave it"), legal threats, reputation threats and walk-away threats. Threats are used to apply pressure, signal non-negotiables, test resolve, and save face. PON says threats follow emotional escalation, and counter-threats raise the temperature. [PON, Threat response at the bargaining table](https://www.pon.harvard.edu/daily/negotiation-skills-daily/negotiation-skills-threat-response-at-the-bargaining-table/)
- **Ultimatums are often not final**: Malhotra (via PON) says people deliver them "out of anger, frustration, or a desire to grab your attention" and not because bargaining room is gone. If ignored, counterparts often respond with concessions; if the same ultimatum is repeated, it is likely the real best-and-final. [PON, Get beyond "take it or leave it"](https://www.pon.harvard.edu/daily/conflict-resolution/get-beyond-take-it-or-leave-it-nb)
- **Going silent / stonewalling**: PON describes it as deliberately withholding information and refusing to communicate to keep leverage; it is a hard-bargaining tactic that carries downside risk when circumstances change. (Illustrated with a sports contract case, not a supplier.) [PON, Stonewalling in negotiations](https://www.pon.harvard.edu/daily/negotiation-skills-daily/the-risks-and-pitfalls-of-stonewalling-in-negotiations/)
- **Anger as a result of low-ball offers**: experiments show stingy concession patterns cause negative attributions about the counterpart's motives and anger, and that this raises the impasse likelihood. Useful for deciding when a vendor "gets frustrated". [Mertes, Kunz and Hüffmeier 2023, abstract page](https://eldorado.tu-dortmund.de/items/f909800c-1d5e-4cb0-9fa9-9745ab154670)
- **Threat of non-supply** appeared in the 2022-23 inflation period: suppliers "threatening non-supply if their claims are not met" (Roland Berger, 1 Mar 2023). [Roland Berger, Managing supplier claims for price increases](https://www.rolandberger.com/en/Insights/Publications/Managing-supplier-claims-for-price-increases-in-the-procurement-process.html)
- **Frustration phrases in real chat** (Craigslist dataset, human-human): a seller response to a lowball was "I am willing to lower the price, but $150 is a little too low" followed by a counter with a side offer. That is the typical mild form of rejection: acknowledge, say it is too low, counter. [He et al. 2018, Table 1](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09637)
- **Limit of evidence**: I found no first-party source with a list of literal supplier phrases such as "not acceptable". The phrases in the simulator section are plain-English renderings of the behaviours above, not quotations.

## 2. What suppliers do when pushed below their margin

- **Compensate through quality, service and R&D**: across 1,700+ purchasing instances over 7 years (Tier 1 automotive suppliers), suppliers "compensate for price concessions" by reducing product quality, service support and R&D toward that buyer in the following year; fair treatment and open communication reduce this. [Carnovale, Henke, DuHadway, Yeniyurt 2019, J. Business Logistics 40(3)](https://pdxscholar.library.pdx.edu/busadmin_fac/151)
- **Reduce scope for a lower price**: US federal rules for best-and-final offers ask offerors for itemised cost proposals "indicating the tasks or scope reductions" that bring costs within budget. This is search-result text of the regulation; I did not open FAR Part 15 itself. **[UNVERIFIED wording]** [FAR Part 15 summary result](https://bureauify.com/guide/far-part-15)
- **Trade, not give**: CIPS frames bargaining as each party giving something up "in return for something back" (tradeables); a concession is giving without return. This is the buyer-side view of what suppliers also do. [CIPS Guidance Notes](https://udntho.files.cmp.optimizely.com/download/assets/CIPS+Negotiation+Tools+Guidance+notes.pdf/490ffa3ebafd11f099f70e9f7034f9df)
- **Value-add instead of price cut**: CIPS notes that when price cannot drop, negotiating value-add such as quality or shorter lead time can make the proposal attractive. [CIPS Guidance Notes](https://udntho.files.cmp.optimizely.com/download/assets/CIPS+Negotiation+Tools+Guidance+notes.pdf/490ffa3ebafd11f099f70e9f7034f9df)
- **Volume, contract length, advance payment, supply-chain finance** are the levers Roland Berger lists for a financially strained supplier: committing to purchase volumes, longer contracts, advance payments. They describe what a strained supplier needs, which is what a vendor in the simulator would ask for. [Roland Berger 2023](https://www.rolandberger.com/en/Insights/Publications/Managing-supplier-claims-for-price-increases-in-the-procurement-process.html)
- **Side offers**: in the Craigslist data sellers traded price for a convenience such as free delivery ("How about $245 and ... I will deliver it to you for free?"). [He et al. 2018, Table 1](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09637)
- **Defending the pricing model**: when buyers propose engineered alternatives or specification changes, suppliers tend to defend their pricing model rather than adapt (Karrass summary). [Karrass, procurement price increases](https://www.karrass.com/blog/procurement-negotiation-price-increases)
- **Reaction to "we have other offers"**: Malhotra and Bazerman research summary (via search result, not opened in full) says negotiators seen as having many alternatives are treated as more attractive partners and are negotiated with less aggressively. Karrass also notes the buyer "phantom competitor" tactic, where the cheaper quote may cover less scope (search-result summary, **[UNVERIFIED]**). A supplier reply, therefore, is to ask for the competing quote's scope and terms. [Search result summary of Malhotra/Bazerman](https://www.pon.harvard.edu/shop/negotiation-geniushow-to-overcome-obstacles-and-achieve-brilliant-results-at-the-bargaining-table-and-beyond/)
- **Reaction to deadlines**: PON's deadline article says a credible, public deadline makes the opponent more likely to concede sooner; secret deadlines lack force. [PON, NBA case and deadlines](https://www.pon.harvard.edu/daily/crisis-negotiations/nba-negotiation-case-illustrates-the-power-of-deadlines/) Karrass says suppliers themselves set "tight deadlines" to force the buyer onto the supplier's timeline. [Karrass](https://www.karrass.com/blog/procurement-negotiation-price-increases)
- **Declining the order**: no source quantifies how often. The nearest evidence is walk-away/BATNA logic: without an alternative a party can be forced into a bad deal; with one they leave. [CIPS Guidance Notes](https://udntho.files.cmp.optimizely.com/download/assets/CIPS+Negotiation+Tools+Guidance+notes.pdf/490ffa3ebafd11f099f70e9f7034f9df)

## 3. Typical supplier counter-tactics

- **Anchoring high / high-ball**: "A supplier rarely starts with their true limit" and begins with "a defensible story and a number that creates room to move". [Karrass](https://www.karrass.com/blog/procurement-negotiation-price-increases) CIPS calls it High Ball Low Ball: open far beyond expectation so later moves look like big concessions. [CIPS Guidance Notes](https://udntho.files.cmp.optimizely.com/download/assets/CIPS+Negotiation+Tools+Guidance+notes.pdf/490ffa3ebafd11f099f70e9f7034f9df)
- **Cost-justification narrative**: raw materials, freight, labour, energy, FX, regulation, capacity; the support is often narrative rather than a transparent breakdown. [Karrass](https://www.karrass.com/blog/procurement-negotiation-price-increases)
- **Headline preservation**: suppliers accept delays or phasing of an increase while keeping the stated number, for perception. [Karrass](https://www.karrass.com/blog/procurement-negotiation-price-increases)
- **Responding to an extreme anchor** (buyer view, informs how vendors expect counters): PON advises saying "we are miles apart on price" before countering; the outcome lands near the midpoint of the first semi-reasonable offer and the counteroffer. [PON, Grappling with anchors](https://www.pon.harvard.edu/daily/dealmaking-daily/dealmaking-grappling-with-anchors-in-negotiation/)
- **Splitting the difference**: eBay data show players prefer to make and accept offers that split the difference between the two most recent offers. [Backus, Blake, Larsen, Tadelis, NBER w24306](https://www.nber.org/papers/w24306.pdf)
- **Bundling / multiple equivalent offers (MESO)**: present two or three packages of equal value to the offerer; recipients see it as cooperative and counter less aggressively; recipients are more susceptible to anchoring by MESOs. [PON, Negotiator's dilemma and MESOs](https://www.pon.harvard.edu/daily/negotiation-skills-daily/managing-the-negotiators-dilemma-nb/)
- **Nibble**: "just before agreement happens, one party asks for one more thing"; the other usually obliges because the deal is near. [CIPS Guidance Notes](https://udntho.files.cmp.optimizely.com/download/assets/CIPS+Negotiation+Tools+Guidance+notes.pdf/490ffa3ebafd11f099f70e9f7034f9df)
- **Good cop / bad cop**: two people from one organisation, one empathic and one harder; PON lists sequential, simultaneous, solo (one person switching) and warning variants. [CIPS](https://udntho.files.cmp.optimizely.com/download/assets/CIPS+Negotiation+Tools+Guidance+notes.pdf/490ffa3ebafd11f099f70e9f7034f9df); [PON, Good cop bad cop](https://www.pon.harvard.edu/daily/batna/the-good-cop-bad-cop-negotiation-strategy/)
- **Snow job and Bogey**: bombard with information; or push hard on one issue so the other agrees more easily on something else. [CIPS](https://udntho.files.cmp.optimizely.com/download/assets/CIPS+Negotiation+Tools+Guidance+notes.pdf/490ffa3ebafd11f099f70e9f7034f9df)
- **Check with my manager**: see section 1; used to delay and to signal that approval was hard. [Karrass](https://www.karrass.com/blog/negotiation-tactic-understanding-authority)
- **False or inflated deadlines**: suppliers set tight deadlines (Karrass above); no source measures how often they are false. Malhotra's view that ultimatums are frequently not final is the closest evidence. [PON](https://www.pon.harvard.edu/daily/conflict-resolution/get-beyond-take-it-or-leave-it-nb)
- **Reciprocity ("I moved, now you move")**: the door-in-the-face sequence is a large first ask, rejection, then a smaller ask that the other side feels obliged to accept. Research (Wong) found that sellers who detected it made more demanding opening offers next time and trusted the buyer less. [PON, Door in the face](https://www.pon.harvard.edu/daily/dispute-resolution/the-door-in-the-face-technique-will-it-backfire-nb/)
- **Reopening agreed terms later**: suppliers attempt to revisit negotiated agreements to test whether documentation holds. [Karrass](https://www.karrass.com/blog/procurement-negotiation-price-increases)
- **Research caution**: a study of professional purchasers found "a hybrid of integrative and distributive tactics in all relationships". Real behaviour mixes cooperative and hard tactics. [Lambrechts, Gelderman, Weelink, Semeijn, IPSERA 2019](https://research.ou.nl/en/publications/how-do-buyers-actually-negotiate-with-their-leverage-and-strategi/)

## 4. Realistic timelines, rounds, and no-deal rates

- **Rounds on a real platform**: eBay Best Offer limits each party to three offers; each offer expires after 48 hours. [Backus et al., NBER w24306](https://www.nber.org/papers/w24306.pdf)
- **Scale and outcomes**: 25.4 million bargaining threads; about one third end in immediate agreement, and most sequences end in disagreement or delayed agreement. Conditional on bargaining, the average sale price was about 73% of list price. [Backus et al. (figures from the paper text and the project summary)](https://www.nber.org/papers/w24306.pdf)
- **Impasse**: another analysis of eBay data (search-result summary, not opened) reports 35% impasse for the median product even when the buyer values the good more than the seller. **[UNVERIFIED]** [Search result, Leuphana "Deal or no deal"](https://fis.leuphana.de/en/publications/deal-or-no-deal-how-round-vs-precise-percentage-offers-and-price-/)
- **Timing of the counteroffer**: across 26 million asynchronous negotiations, later buyer counteroffers led to better final prices and lower impasse risk; ambitious counteroffers lowered price but raised impasse risk. For a simulator: a vendor taking some time to reply is normal, and a very aggressive counter increases walk-away probability. [Teichmann et al., Group Decision and Negotiation 34(4), 2025](https://fis.leuphana.de/en/publications/optimizing-counteroffers-how-timing-and-magnitude-shape-sale-pric/)
- **Round vs precise offers**: round-percentage offers of the list price carried a markedly smaller impasse risk than just-above or just-below values (search-result summary, **[UNVERIFIED]**). [Leuphana](https://fis.leuphana.de/en/publications/deal-or-no-deal-how-round-vs-precise-percentage-offers-and-price-/)
- **Chat length**: human-human Craigslist price negotiations averaged 9.2 turns per dialogue (both sides combined) in 6,682 dialogues. [He et al. 2018, Table 3](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09637)
- **Procurement process length**: Victoria's guide says "negotiations can be time consuming" and should be weighed against the value gained; it recommends a BATNA and describes a BAFO "which will not be subject to subsequent negotiations". It gives no hours or days. [Victorian Government negotiation guide](https://www.buyingfor.vic.gov.au/negotiate-goods-and-services-procurement-guide)
- **Not found**: reliable data on typical B2B supplier reply times (hours/days) and B2B no-deal percentages. The simulator should treat its delay and no-deal numbers as assumptions.

## 5. Differences by supplier type and market conditions

- **Kraljic / relationship type decides style**: strategic items lead to collaboration; a monopoly supplier holds the power and the buyer ends up accommodating; routine items in perfect competition suit a competitive style, often with the "take it or leave it" ploy when the relationship is not valued. [CIPS Guidance Notes, section 5](https://udntho.files.cmp.optimizely.com/download/assets/CIPS+Negotiation+Tools+Guidance+notes.pdf/490ffa3ebafd11f099f70e9f7034f9df)
- **Lever vs strategic vs locked-in**: the IPSERA study found both integrative and distributive tactics in all three relationship types, so a strategic supplier is not automatically soft. [Lambrechts et al. 2019](https://research.ou.nl/en/publications/how-do-buyers-actually-negotiate-with-their-leverage-and-strategi/)
- **Supplier financial health changes what they accept**: Roland Berger separates suppliers lacking flexibility (they want volume commitments, longer contracts, advance payment) from suppliers with room (buyers can push, accelerate, extend payment terms). [Roland Berger 2023](https://www.rolandberger.com/en/Insights/Publications/Managing-supplier-claims-for-price-increases-in-the-procurement-process.html)
- **Market conditions**: in tight, inflationary markets suppliers threatened non-supply and pushed price increases; when prices fall, buyers claw back (same Roland Berger piece). In scrap, price negotiations are tied to indices (Argus, AMM), and mills try to recoup when finished-steel prices fall. [Argus scrap markets](https://argusmedia.com/metals/argus-scrap-markets) (search-result text only, not opened in full **[UNVERIFIED detail]**).
- **Scrap / recyclers**: contracts commonly use a percentage of an index price, so the haggle is on the percentage and on weighing, pickup and payment terms, not a freely invented price. Based on search results from government tender listings and Argus; I found no first-party description of recycler chat behaviour. **[UNVERIFIED]** [Argus](https://argusmedia.com/metals/argus-scrap-markets)
- **Services**: scope and SLA are the main trade items; BAFO guidance asks for itemised scope reductions. See section 2. **[Services-specific behaviour data not found]**

## 6. Indian B2B context

- **MSME payment cap**: Section 15 of the MSMED Act 2006 requires payment within the agreed period and in any case within 45 days of acceptance (agreement period cannot exceed 45 days; 15 days if there is no written agreement, per secondary sources). [LiveLaw article by Jog Singh, updated 24 Nov 2025](https://www.livelaw.in/amp/law-firms/law-firm-articles-/the-stringency-of-interest-provisions-under-msmed-act-implementation-and-judicial-interpretation-310929)
- **Interest on delay**: Section 16 imposes compound interest with monthly rests at three times the RBI bank rate, "notwithstanding anything contained in any agreement". [LiveLaw](https://www.livelaw.in/amp/law-firms/law-firm-articles-/the-stringency-of-interest-provisions-under-msmed-act-implementation-and-judicial-interpretation-310929)
- **Tax angle (s.43B(h))**: sums owed to micro or small enterprises beyond the section 15 limit are deductible only on actual payment. The Finance Ministry says the aim is to ease MSME cash-flow and working-capital costs. It also did not address concerns about order cancellations or suppliers dropping registration. [TaxGuru, Finance Ministry clarification](https://taxguru.in/income-tax/finance-ministry-clarifies-section-43bh-45-day-msme-payment-rule.html) The 15-day rule and effective date (1 April 2024) come from secondary sites in search results. **[UNVERIFIED primary]** [Busy explainer](https://busy.in/tds/section-43bh-msme-payment-rule-and-45-day-limit-explained/)
- **Payment delays are common**: the Ministry of MSME's SAMADHAAN portal records pending applications; search results gave about Rs 21,583.7 crore (to 22 July 2024) and about Rs 31,039 crore (Dec 2025), and a GAME/D&B estimate of INR 10.7 lakh crore locked up annually. I did not open these; treat as indicative. **[UNVERIFIED]** [Rajya Sabha reply listing](https://rsdebate.nic.in/bitstream/123456789/739827/1/PQ_259_06022023_U427_p296_p296.pdf); [D&B India release](https://dnb.co.in/media/press-release-details/2052)
- **Culture**: an academic study (Cortes, Chapa, Dania, 2011) proposes that power distance sets the stage for negotiation in India and respect for social formalities builds long-term relationships. [IBER 10(4)](https://clutejournals.com/index.php/IBER/article/view/4185) Other summaries (relationship over short-term gain, indirect communication, long negotiations) came from search-result snippets only and I could not open the primary papers (one returned 403). **[UNVERIFIED]** [Emerald JMD article, not opened](https://emeraldinsight.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/JMD-07-2019-0298/full/html)
- **Not found**: any peer-reviewed or official source describing Indian vendor phrasing, reply times, or how often Indian suppliers refuse orders. Say "none found" for those.

## 7. Published transcripts and example dialogues

- **CraigslistBargain** (He et al., EMNLP 2018): 6,682 human-human dialogues, average 9.2 turns, with coarse dialogue acts (greet, inquire, inform, propose, counter, insist, agree, offer, accept). Persuasion techniques observed: embellishment, side offers, appeals to sympathy. The data are public; I did not open the dataset itself. [He et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09637)
- **Example message structure** (used TV, listing $275, buyer target $192; paraphrased from Table 1):
  1. Buyer greets (greet). 2. Seller greets, confirms availability (greet). 3. Buyer asks about condition (inquire). 4. Seller describes the item favourably (inform). 5. Buyer says the price is high and proposes $150 (propose). 6. Seller says "willing to lower" but $150 is too low, counters $245 plus free delivery (counter). 7. Buyer argues age and counters $225 with delivery (counter). 8. Seller accepts (agree). 9. Buyer thanks (agree). 10. Seller enters formal offer $225 (offer). 11. Buyer accepts (accept). [He et al. 2018, Table 1](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.09637)
- **Structure insight**: information exchange first, then a lowball, then a counter that moves only part of the way and adds a side benefit, then a counter near the midpoint, then acceptance. The final $225 is about 82% of list and about 46% of the way from the first buyer offer.
- **eBay Best Offer threads**: alternating offers with up to three each and 48-hour expiry; the data include accept, counter, decline sequences. Raw message text is not part of the open data. [Backus et al.](https://www.nber.org/papers/w24306.pdf)
- **Not found**: a published full transcript of a B2B supplier price negotiation (procurement). Harvard teaching cases exist but are paywalled; I did not obtain any.

## What a simulator should do

Each rule: state, trigger, message intent, probability or ordering rule, short example, and the supporting finding. Probabilities are design suggestions inferred from the findings, not measured values.

1. **Opening anchor** (state: OPENING). Trigger: first offer or quote request. Intent: quote above target with a cost story. Order: always; opening margin set so the vendor has room to move (typically a fixed band above its walk-away price). Example: "Our price is X per unit; raw material and freight have gone up." Support: anchoring and justification narrative (Karrass; CIPS High Ball).
2. **Acknowledge and counter** (state: BARGAINING). Trigger: buyer offer above the vendor's floor but below its ask. Intent: say the offer is too low and counter with a smaller step than the buyer made. Order: concession size shrinks each round (for example 50% then 35% then 20% of the remaining gap); moves toward the midpoint of the last two offers when buyer moved this round. Example: "That's a little low for me, I can come down to X." Support: Craigslist Table 1; split-the-difference norm (Backus et al.); midpoint rule (PON anchoring).
3. **Concession for concession** (state: BARGAINING). Trigger: vendor is about to move. Intent: tie the move to a buyer commitment (volume, faster payment, longer contract, pickup). Order: used at least once before the final price move; skipped if the buyer already offered a trade. Example: "I can do X if you take the full lot." Support: trading rather than giving (CIPS bargaining stage); reciprocity.
4. **Side offer instead of price** (state: BARGAINING). Trigger: buyer pushes below the vendor's comfortable zone but above the hard floor. Intent: offer delivery, pickup, scope, or payment-term changes rather than price. Order: preferred over the third consecutive price cut. Example: "Price stays at X, but I'll include delivery." Support: Craigslist side offers; CIPS value-add.
5. **Mild frustration** (state: RESISTING). Trigger: buyer makes a lowball far below the floor, or two or more small moves in a row. Intent: express annoyance, restate that the offer is too far away, ask for a serious number. Order: probability rises with how far below the floor the offer is and with repeated stingy moves. Example: "That's far from where I can go. I need a realistic number to continue." Support: stingy concessions create anger and impasse (Mertes et al.); PON anchoring advice ("miles apart").
6. **Check with manager / delay** (state: PAUSED). Trigger: buyer asks for a price below the vendor's first concession band, or a big concession would be next. Intent: delay and signal difficulty. Order: at most once or twice per conversation; reply later with a modest improvement or a refusal. Example: "Let me check with my manager and get back to you." Support: CIPS lack of authority; Karrass.
7. **Mother Hubbard** (state: RESISTING). Trigger: vendor is near its floor. Intent: claim nothing is left to give. Order: appears before the real floor, then the vendor still moves a small step if the buyer holds (so it is not always final). Example: "I've really got nothing left to give on price." Support: CIPS Mother Hubbard.
8. **Deadline or validity window** (state: CLOSING). Trigger: vendor wants to close, or buyer stalls. Intent: add a time limit on the price or stock. Order: attach to the vendor's last or second-to-last offer; sometimes extended once if the buyer replies politely. Example: "This price holds until tonight, then I'll need to re-quote." Support: Karrass (tight deadlines); PON on deadlines; Malhotra (ultimatums often not final).
9. **Take it or leave it** (state: FINAL). Trigger: vendor is at or within a small margin of its floor, or after repeated lowballs. Intent: state the offer is final. Order: one use; if the buyer ignores it and offers a small move, concede a very small step with probability (for example 30%), otherwise repeat it. A repeated final offer is then treated as real. Example: "X is my final price. Take it or leave it." Support: CIPS; PON/Malhotra (ignored ultimatums often followed by concessions; repeated ones are real).
10. **Competing offers claim** (state: BARGAINING). Trigger: buyer says another vendor is cheaper. Intent: ask about the other offer's scope, quantity and terms; then either hold, offer a small matching concession, or let the buyer go. Order: if the buyer's claim has no specifics, hold price and ask; if specifics are given and the price is above the vendor's floor, move once. Example: "What does their quote include? Is it the same grade and delivery?" Support: Malhotra and Bazerman on alternatives; Karrass phantom competitor (unverified).
11. **Below floor: re-scope, not accept** (state: RESISTING). Trigger: buyer offer below the floor. Intent: propose smaller quantity, lower grade, or extra charge for delivery, instead of just refusing. Order: first response below the floor; refuse outright only if the buyer repeats it. Example: "At that price I can only do a lower grade or a smaller lot." Support: suppliers compensate with quality and service (Carnovale et al.); scope reduction in BAFO guidance (unverified wording).
12. **Surcharge or condition** (state: BARGAINING). Trigger: buyer asks for faster payment terms or special delivery on top of a low price. Intent: add a surcharge, advance payment or minimum volume. Order: applies when buyer asks for two or more concessions at once. Example: "With 60-day payment, the price goes up by X." Support: Roland Berger (advance payment, volume, longer contracts as supplier needs).
13. **Nibble at the end** (state: CLOSING). Trigger: agreement within one step. Intent: ask for one small extra (free pickup, advance, rounding). Order: low probability (for example 15-25%), once, accept if the buyer declines. Example: "Fine at X, can you cover the loading?" Support: CIPS Nibble.
14. **Going silent** (state: STALLED). Trigger: buyer sends repeated lowballs after a final offer, or the vendor is walking away. Intent: no reply for a while, then a short closing message. Order: used rarely and before a walk-away; shows as a longer reply delay. Example: (no reply), then "I'll leave it there unless you can meet X." Support: PON stonewalling; Teichmann et al. (late replies are normal in asynchronous bargaining).
15. **Walk away** (state: ENDED). Trigger: buyer stays below the floor after the final offer, or insults, or the anger level crosses a threshold. Intent: end politely, leave the door open. Order: probability rises with anger (rule 5) and with distance below floor. Example: "I can't make that work. If your budget changes, let me know." Support: BATNA logic (CIPS, Victoria guide); more ambitious counteroffers raise impasse risk (Teichmann et al.).
16. **Round limits and pace** (global). Use short chats: a typical completed deal of 3 to 6 vendor offers and about 9 messages total; allow longer for bundled terms. Reply delays vary (minutes to hours, with "check with manager" longer). Example: n/a. Support: Craigslist average 9.2 turns; eBay three offers each, 48-hour expiry. The hours and days are design assumptions, not research results.
17. **Bundle options** (state: BARGAINING). Trigger: buyer asks for multiple terms. Intent: offer two or three packages of similar value to the vendor. Order: when two or more terms are open, once. Example: "Option A: X with 30 days; Option B: lower X with advance." Support: MESO (PON).
18. **Supplier type modifiers**. Commodity or routine item with many sellers: more willing to give small moves, quicker to say "take it or leave it", index-linked pricing. Specialised or monopoly-like item: slower concessions, more "manager check", holds price longer. Financially stressed vendor: asks for advance, volume, faster payment. Strong vendor: more deadlines and surcharges. Support: CIPS Kraljic styles; Roland Berger.
19. **India modifiers (optional)**. If the vendor is flagged as an MSME: payment terms beyond 45 days are refused or priced higher, and the vendor mentions the 45-day rule. Relationship wording (courtesy, "long-term business") in the early and closing messages. Support: MSMED Act s.15/16, s.43B(h); Cortes et al. (cultural claim thin).
20. **No immediate accept at the first fair offer**. About one third of real threads end in immediate agreement, so a vendor should usually counter once even if the offer is acceptable; accept immediately only if the offer is at or above its target. Support: Backus et al.
